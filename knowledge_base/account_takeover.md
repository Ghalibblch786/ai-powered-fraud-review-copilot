# Account Takeover

## What it is
Account takeover (ATO) happens when a fraudster gains control of a legitimate customer's account or credentials and begins transacting as if they were the real owner. Because the person behind the account has changed, their behavior often diverges from the account's established pattern — most tellingly, in what kinds of merchants they use.

## How it typically shows up
A real customer tends to have consistent habits — shopping in the same handful of merchant categories over time. When an account is taken over, the new user has no knowledge of or interest in matching that pattern, so purchases start appearing in categories the account has never touched before. A customer with years of grocery and dining history suddenly making an electronics or travel purchase is a classic takeover signature — notably, these transactions can look perfectly reasonable in size, which is what makes the category shift itself the meaningful signal rather than the amount.

## Evidence signals
- A transaction in a merchant category with no prior history on that account
- A sudden break from the account's established category preferences
- The shift happening without necessarily any single large or unusual amount — takeover activity can look ordinary in size while still being behaviorally inconsistent
- The new category appearing without any clear transition (e.g. no gradual shift in habits leading up to it)

## Innocent explanations
- People try new kinds of shops all the time: a first doctor's visit, a holiday booking, a gift.
- The flag fires as soon as a customer with only a couple of past purchases buys in a category not seen before. With a short history, many ordinary purchases look "new".
- A new category on its own, with a normal amount, at a low- or medium-risk merchant, at the home location, is weak evidence.

## What to check
- How many past transactions the customer has (a short history makes "new category" a weak signal).
- The amount compared with the customer's typical spending range.
- The merchant's risk tier.
- The location compared with the customer's home location.
- The account's age.
- Any other purchases close in time.

## How to decide
- Lean **clear** when the new category is the only unusual thing: normal amount, low- or medium-risk merchant, home location, established account.
- Lean **escalate** when the new category comes with another warning sign: a high-risk merchant, an amount above the typical range, a different location, a burst of purchases, or a very new account (under about 60 days). Strictly speaking, a very new account points more to new-account fraud than to takeover, but it still deserves a closer look.
- Describe evidence as "consistent with" a pattern. Never state that fraud happened; the analyst makes the final decision.
